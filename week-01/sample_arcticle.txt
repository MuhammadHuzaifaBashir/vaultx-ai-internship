Ransomware attacks have shifted significantly over the past few years, moving away from opportunistic, mass-scale infections toward highly targeted operations against specific organizations. Attackers now spend weeks or even months inside a network before deploying their payload, using that time to map out backup systems, identify the most sensitive data, and disable security tools that might detect them. This "double extortion" model, where attackers both encrypt data and threaten to leak it publicly, has made simply restoring from backups insufficient as a defense, since the reputational and legal damage from a data leak persists even after systems are recovered.

Small and mid-sized businesses have become increasingly common targets, largely because they often lack dedicated security teams while still holding valuable data such as customer records or payment information. Many of these organizations only invest in cybersecurity after experiencing a breach, at which point the costs of incident response, legal fees, and regulatory fines can be far higher than the cost of preventive measures would have been. Cyber insurance has grown in response, but insurers now require proof of basic security controls, like multi-factor authentication and regular backups, before offering coverage.

On the defense side, security teams have leaned heavily into automation to keep pace with the speed and scale of modern attacks. Manual log review is no longer feasible given the volume of data generated by even a modest-sized network, so tools that use pattern detection to flag suspicious behavior automatically have become essential. However, these tools are only as good as the rules and models behind them, which means skilled analysts are still needed to investigate alerts, tune detection systems, and respond when something slips through.

For students entering the field, this creates a clear takeaway: technical skills like scripting and system administration matter, but so does the ability to think like an attacker, understand business risk, and communicate findings clearly to non-technical stakeholders who ultimately decide how much to invest in security.
